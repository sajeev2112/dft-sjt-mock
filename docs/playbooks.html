Theme playbooks

For each theme: what the keys usually reward, what they usually mark down, and the nuance that changes the order. They describe patterns, not a script: each question's own facts decide.

Candour & your own errors

17 practice questions.

Usually ranks high

  • Make the patient safe and limit harm
  • Tell them promptly, plainly and specifically, with an apology (an apology isn’t an admission of liability)
  • Put it right, or arrange for someone who can
  • Write accurate notes, with late entries dated and nothing altered
  • Report it as an incident and check whether anyone else is affected
  • Inform your ES and reflect

Usually ranks low

  • Concealing it, or waiting to see if they notice
  • Altering, deleting or tidying records
  • Blaming a colleague, or asking the nurse to keep quiet
  • Calling your defence organisation before telling the patient

Nuance: In “immediate” questions, urgent safety steps come before informing your ES or reflecting.

Raising concerns about colleagues

19 practice questions.

Usually ranks high

  • Act on any immediate risk, discreetly
  • Speak to the colleague privately and directly first
  • Give them the chance to correct things or self-report
  • If it’s repeated or serious, go up one rung: ES, practice manager, owner or lead
  • Make factual notes, and think about which patients were affected

Usually ranks low

  • Staying silent, covering for them, or just “keeping an eye”
  • Confronting them in public, gossiping or posting in group chats
  • Covert workarounds, such as asking the nurse to swap items
  • Going to the GDC before any local route

Nuance: You don’t need proof to raise a concern, and the duty overrides loyalty. If a quiet word has already failed, don’t repeat it; move up a rung.

Consent & capacity

22 practice questions.

Usually ranks high

  • Presume capacity and support the decision (simpler explanations, visual aids)
  • Explore their reasons before you advise
  • Explain risks, benefits, alternatives (including doing nothing) and costs
  • Respect a competent decision and record it
  • Under-16s: assess Gillick competence
  • Lacking capacity: follow the best-interests process, and inform an LPA attorney and work with them

Usually ranks low

  • Pressure, coercion or scaring them
  • Carrying on after a refusal
  • Accepting consent from someone without legal authority
  • Treating an unwise choice as incapacity
  • Giving irreversible treatment just because it was asked for, or overriding a valid attorney

Nuance: Autonomy lets patients refuse. It doesn’t oblige you to provide treatment you believe is against their interests; offer a second opinion instead.

Confidentiality & information

19 practice questions.

Usually ranks high

  • No disclosure without consent, and that includes confirming attendance and costs
  • A kind refusal, plus a way forward (offer to pass on a message)
  • A private word with whoever breached it, plus a system fix
  • Get posts taken down fast
  • For disclosure decisions: consent, then the law, then a serious public interest

Usually ranks low

  • Public replies to reviews or posts
  • Screenshots or anything that spreads the information further
  • Disclosing to keep the peace or save time
  • Proof of identity treated as a right to information

Nuance: Confidentiality gives way to safeguarding, serious crime or a legal requirement, and then only the minimum necessary is shared.

Safeguarding & vulnerable patients

18 practice questions.

Usually ranks high

  • Meet the immediate clinical need
  • Record findings and what was said, objectively
  • Involve the practice safeguarding lead promptly
  • 999 if someone is in immediate danger
  • With adults who have capacity: support them and seek their agreement

Usually ranks low

  • Investigating yourself, or examining beyond your clinical remit
  • Confronting the suspected abuser
  • Waiting for proof, or for the next appointment
  • Promising secrecy
  • Sharing information outside the proper process

Nuance: A child’s welfare is paramount. For competent adults, consent is central unless someone is in immediate danger or others are at risk.

Working within competence & safe conditions

19 practice questions.

Usually ranks high

  • Don’t start what you can’t do safely
  • Get on-site supervision, or defer the complex part
  • Work with a trained nurse; look after them if they’re unwell
  • Stop using equipment that has failed a test
  • Stay within your scope, including prescribing
  • Be honest with the patient if you need to rebook

Usually ranks low

  • Going ahead on theory, or “the ES said I’d be fine”
  • Working without a second person when nothing about the situation is exceptional
  • Untrained helpers, or “re-test and carry on”
  • Disclaimers presented as a fix

Nuance: Refusing all new, supervised work is wrong too. DFT exists to extend your competence safely.

Communication & complaints

20 practice questions.

Usually ranks high

  • Pause, acknowledge feelings, explore before fixing
  • Examine and find the cause
  • Give an honest explanation and agree a plan
  • Use a professional interpreter for anything important
  • Take criticism offline and treat it as a complaint

Usually ranks low

  • Dismissive or lecturing responses
  • Defensiveness, or offering the complaints leaflet as a first move
  • Handing the conversation to someone else
  • “You’d have to pay privately for better”
  • Threats, or public replies

Nuance: Stay calm with angry patients and protect everyone’s dignity. Threatening the police is only right if there’s violence.

Prioritising & workload

18 practice questions.

Usually ranks high

  • Clinical urgency first; send red flags to hospital
  • Tell waiting patients, apologise and offer choices
  • Raise workload directly with whoever is causing it
  • Involve your ES in problems built into the system
  • Make realistic plans

Usually ranks low

  • Cutting clinical corners to catch up
  • Quietly withdrawing help, so patients lose out
  • Moving your load onto colleagues
  • Cancelling everything, which is disproportionate

Nuance: Routinely working through lunch or staying late is “proactive but”: it sits in the middle, above secrecy or corner-cutting.

Team relationships & feedback

19 practice questions.

Usually ranks high

  • A private, direct conversation, assuming good intent
  • Explain why it matters, and offer support or training
  • A practice-meeting fix
  • Accept feedback and ask for specifics
  • Own your behaviour and repair it
  • Challenge discrimination, whoever it comes from

Usually ranks low

  • Public correction or threats
  • Trying to find out who complained
  • Swapping staff to avoid the issue
  • Gossip, blame, or standing by

Nuance: Disciplining staff isn’t an FD’s role. Support colleagues and point them to the proper routes.

Health & wellbeing

17 practice questions.

Usually ranks high

  • Tell your TPD or ES early
  • Get occupational health advice rather than judging your own risk
  • Don’t work if you’re infectious or impaired
  • Follow the sharps policy straight away
  • A colleague at risk: ask directly, get help today, be honest about the limits of confidentiality

Usually ranks low

  • Soldiering on, or hiding it
  • Assessing your own risk
  • Minimising someone’s distress
  • Delaying help until later in the week

Nuance: Looking after your health is part of patient safety, not a sign of weakness.

Probity: honesty, records & rules

19 practice questions.

Usually ranks high

  • Refuse to collude, and explain why calmly
  • Honest, factual letters, records and references
  • Decline shortcuts and offer a proper alternative
  • Encourage self-correction before escalating
  • Record unusual requests in the notes

Usually ranks low

  • Backdating or fabricating anything
  • Vague wording or omissions meant to mislead
  • Accepting a false sign-off, even “with a note”
  • Being signed in by someone else, or fake reviews

Nuance: Dishonesty is the category most likely to end a career, even when the underlying issue was minor.

Money, NHS & private care

17 practice questions.

Usually ranks high

  • Present every option, including NHS care, neutrally
  • A written plan showing costs and NHS or private status
  • Let the patient’s values decide
  • Set recalls and claims by clinical need and the rules
  • Challenge target pressure calmly; check the rules if unsure

Usually ranks low

  • Saying a treatment is only available privately when it isn’t
  • Pressure, or assuming what they can afford
  • Splitting claims, or complying to hit targets
  • Reporting before you’ve discussed it

Nuance: The claims you submit are your responsibility, whoever asked you to make them.
